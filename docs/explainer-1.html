A one-page explainer

Indivisa in Plain English

A company borrows money from a lot of people. Every year it owes them all a small thank-you payment. Indivisa pays them all with one button, all at the same instant, and nobody sees anyone else's line. Here is the whole story, from the very start.

Part one · Someone needs money

Northwind Rail wants to build a train line

It will cost $50 million. No single bank wants to lend that much to one company, so Northwind does what companies and governments have done for hundreds of years: it borrows a little from a lot of people.

It creates 50,000 IOUs of $1,000 each. Anyone can buy one. Buy one and you have lent Northwind $1,000. In return, Northwind promises you two things:

  1. Every year on 1 December, it pays you a thank-you of $40. That is 4% of your $1,000.
  2. On 1 December 2031, it gives your $1,000 back.

That IOU is called a bond. Whoever holds it is a holder. Northwind, the borrower, is the issuer. In our story the 50,000 bonds end up with about 500 holders: some people own one bond, some pension funds own hundreds.

Part two · The thank-you payment

1 December comes round

Northwind now owes $40 for every one of its 50,000 bonds. That is $2 million, going out to 500 different holders, on one day.

Northwind does not do this itself. It hires a bank, the paying agent, and sends it the $2 million. The paying agent looks at the register, the list of who holds how many bonds, works out what each holder is owed, and pays them.

Today that means spreadsheets, files sent from bank to bank, and a lot of checking. Mistakes are common enough that the industry has a number for them: its own research says roughly 3 to 10 in every 100 of these events go wrong somewhere, and fixing them costs a typical bank millions a year.

The cast

Who is who

The issuer

Northwind Rail. It borrowed the money and owes the coupon. On payday it sends one lump sum to the paying agent and its job is done.

Sees: its own payment to the paying agent

The holders

Maya, Omar, the Pine Pension Fund and 497 others. They lent the money. Each is owed a coupon. How much they lent is nobody else's business.

Sees: only their own line

The paying agent

The bank Northwind hired to hand out the money. It reads the register, does the maths, and presses the button.

Sees: the whole list, because that is its job

The register

Not a person: the list. Who holds how many bonds, frozen on an agreed day (the record date) so everyone agrees exactly who gets paid.

Kept private. Never published.

The cash registry

The system that runs the money itself, a bit like the central bank behind a currency. It confirms every movement of its own currency.

Sees: every movement of its currency, nothing else

The ledger (Canton)

The shared record book everyone trusts because nobody can secretly change it. Its special trick: each person only sees the pages that concern them.

Shows each party only its own pages
The words

Six words that carry the whole thing

Bond
An IOU. A big loan chopped into thousands of equal pieces so many people can each lend a bit.
Coupon
The yearly thank-you payment on a bond. Named after the paper tickets you used to cut off.
Corporate action
Any event where a company does something to everyone who holds its bonds or shares at once: paying a coupon, paying a dividend, paying the loan back. Coupons are our example; the others work the same way.
Payment, or leg
One holder's slice of the payout. "Maya's leg" is the $40 going to Maya.
Transaction
One entry in the record book. Normally one entry means one payment. A batch is many payments packed into one entry.
Atomic
All or nothing. Either the whole batch goes into the book, or none of it does. Never half.
The steps

How one coupon gets paid

  1. Sign up, once

    Each holder signs one agreement with the paying agent. It is like giving your bank details to a new employer. After this, the holder never has to do anything again, for this coupon or any future one.

  2. Payday is announced

    Northwind says: "On 1 December we pay $40 per bond to everyone on the register as of 15 November."

  3. The paying agent does the maths

    It reads the register and works out each holder's amount. It checks that all the pennies add up to exactly the $2 million Northwind sent. If they are off by one cent, nothing goes out.

    Maya · 1 bond · $40
    Omar · 5 bonds · $200
    Pine Pension Fund · 310 bonds · $12,400

  4. The money is lined up

    For each holder, the paying agent sets aside the exact amount and, using the sign-up from step 1, marks it "ready to be received by Maya". This happens holder by holder in the background. It can take a while, and that is fine.

  5. One button

    The paying agent sends a single instruction to the ledger: pay all of these, now. That is one entry in the record book, however many holders there are.

  6. All or nothing

    If even one payment cannot go through, say one holder never signed up, the ledger throws out the whole batch. Nobody is paid, nothing is half done. The paying agent fixes the problem and presses again.

  7. Everyone checks their own page

    Maya sees $40 arrived. She cannot see Omar's $200 or the Pine Fund's $12,400. She cannot even see that they exist.

The striped rows are not "hidden" data. On Maya's page they simply do not exist. Those amounts were never delivered to her, so there is nothing on her side to leak or forward by accident.

Why this needs Canton

On a normal blockchain, everything is public

The moment the coupon was paid, the whole register, who lent how much, would be on the internet for anyone to read. In most markets that would breach the confidentiality a register is held under, and it is commercially damaging everywhere. Canton lets one bank pay five hundred people in one entry while each of them sees only their own line. That is the whole point of Indivisa.
