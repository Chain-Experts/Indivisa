The problem03 / 15

And it cannot simply move onto a public chain.

Paying a coupon publicly publishes the register. Every holder's position size becomes readable by every other holder, by their competitors, and by anyone else, at the moment the coupon pays.

  • A bondholder register is not public information, and in many markets is protected by data-protection or banking-secrecy rules.
  • It is commercially sensitive in all of them: it is the client list.
  • That is why this workflow has not moved to a transparent ledger.

What it does05 / 15

The value, in the operations manager's terms

The run is finished when the transaction commits.

The event closes the same day
Not after working the 29% of income events that do not go straight through, on Canton’s own published figures. The transaction commits, and that is the answer.
The exception queue is empty
There is no partial state to work. The class of exception "some paid, some not" stops existing.
Nothing to chase next week
No claims tail, no reconciliation against four systems, no explaining to the issuer what happened.

And the register never leaves the paying agent.

What it does06 / 15

Four steps, and only the last one moves money.

  1. Once, at onboarding: each holder signs one standing agreement with the paying agent.
  2. Record date: the register is snapshotted and entitlements calculated, rounded so the parts sum to the announced total exactly.
  3. Before payment: the agent sets aside the cash and prepares one allocation per holder.
  4. Payment date: one settlement. Every leg, or none.

Holders authorise once, at onboarding. Never per coupon.

Why now07 / 15

Token Standard V2 made this possible in June.

CIP-0112, approved 12 June 2026: multi-leg settlement where the executor sees every leg and each participant sees only its own. Its worked example is trading and netting. CIP-112 did not design this. It enabled it.

Control11 / 15

In institutional systems, four-eyes authorisation is a requirement, not a feature.

A run can name an approver: a decentralised party, through BitSafe's Decentralization Manager. One optional field. The product is unchanged without it.

One approval→the ledger refuses
Two of three→settles

The threshold is enforced by the ledger, not by our code. In the local demo all three nodes are ours; on DevNet the second is BitSafe's.

Honest12 / 15

What we are not claiming.

Real

The ledger, the contracts, the settlement, the refusal, the privacy, the DevNet update id, the governance engine and its threshold. Every number on screen.

Simulated

The cash is TestTokenV2, the standard's own reference asset, not Canton Coin, chosen because it is signed by owner and admin, so it forces the same receiver-authorisation case. Holders and positions are generated. Three approver nodes on one machine are not three independent operators.

Not attempted

Corporate-action announcement data. Chainlink, with DTCC and Swift, is attacking that layer. We do the payment layer, and saying so makes the position sharper.

We would rather tell you this than have you find it.

Honest13 / 15

Known, designed, not yet built

Scoping a run is the first thing we build next.

If 5 of 250 holders are not ready, the batch refuses for all 250. A paying agent needs to settle the 245 today and carry the 5 into a second run. The batch stays all-or-nothing; its membership becomes a decision, not an accident. The model supports it. The workflow, and the audit trail that says why five were held back, are not written.

  • The set-up surface: the console settles a run; it does not keep the register. Holders, positions and the schedule come from systems a paying agent already runs, and in the demo a script stands in for them. That boundary is deliberate — it is why Indivisa works against registers that already exist.
  • Production onboarding: holders sign through a real client, not a script.
  • The scheduled agent: today a person presses the button; in production a daemon watches payment dates.
  • Real cash: a production run settles Canton Coin or a registry's own token. The model never names the asset, so this is a field and one registry adapter, not a rewrite.
  • MainNet: the DevNet run proves the path; MainNet is a deployment decision, not a code change. We run validator infrastructure, and it is the first thing after submission.
